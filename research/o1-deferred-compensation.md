# Deferring Compensation While Maintaining O-1 Employment — Evidence and Authority

*Research memo. Compiled from primary sources (statute, 8 CFR, USCIS Policy Manual, USCIS
guidance, DOL regulations) plus secondary practitioner commentary. Not legal advice — the
material-change analysis in Part 3 should be run by an immigration attorney before any
change takes effect.*

---

**Companion memo:** `valid-o1-status-sources.md` — the sources defining valid O-1 and valid
nonimmigrant status generally. Read together: that memo lists every element of maintaining
status, and none of them is compensation.

---

## 1. Bottom line

There is **no requirement anywhere in the O-1 statute, regulations, or USCIS policy that an
O-1 beneficiary be paid any particular amount, on any particular schedule, or at all.**
O-1 status attaches to *performing the services described in the approved petition for the
petitioning employer* — not to receiving cash for them.

The affirmative case rests on two things:

1. an **express USCIS statement** that no wage structure is required; and
2. the **absence** of any payment obligation in 8 CFR 214.2(o) — an absence that is
   meaningful because Congress and DOL wrote exactly such an obligation into the H-1B rules
   and did not write one into the O rules.

The real constraints are not "must you be paid" but (a) whether the deferral is a **material
change** requiring an amended I-129, (b) whether the original petition **relied on the
high-salary criterion**, (c) whether the employment stays **bona fide and documented**, and
(d) **wage-and-hour and tax law**, which is where most of the actual legal risk sits.

## 2. Direct authority that no pay level is required

### 2.1 USCIS says so expressly

USCIS, *O Nonimmigrant Classifications: Question and Answers*:

> "The regulations do not contain a prevailing wage requirement. Furthermore, **no particular
> wage structure is required.**"

and, on what the petition's contract evidence must show:

> "A detailed description of the wage offered or fee structure and that the wage offered/fee
> structure was agreed upon may satisfy this requirement."

This is the single most useful sentence in the record. It establishes that the **wage term is
whatever the parties agreed to**, and that USCIS's interest is in *what was offered and
accepted*, not in the size or timing of the number.

### 2.2 The regulation keys status to services, not to pay

8 CFR 214.2(o)(1)(i):

> "Under section 101(a)(15)(O) of the Act, a qualified alien may be authorized to come to the
> United States **to perform services** relating to an event or events if petitioned for by an
> employer."

8 CFR 214.1(e) (maintenance of status):

> "Any other nonimmigrant in the United States may not engage in any employment unless he has
> been accorded a nonimmigrant classification which authorizes employment... A nonimmigrant who
> is permitted to engage in employment **may engage only in such employment as has been
> authorized.** Any unauthorized employment by a nonimmigrant constitutes a failure to maintain
> status..."

The status condition is *which* work you do, for *whom*. Compensation is not a condition of
maintaining status.

### 2.3 The only three places 8 CFR 214.2(o) mentions pay at all

A word-by-word scan of the complete text of 8 CFR 214.2(o) turns up exactly three
pay-related references, none of which imposes a payment obligation on an employer-petitioner:

| Cite | Text | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| (o)(2)(iv)(E)(*1*) | "An agent performing the function of an employer must provide the contractual agreement between the agent and the beneficiary which **specifies the wage offered** and the other terms and conditions of employment" | A *disclosure* rule, and only for agent-filed petitions. It requires stating the wage, not paying a set one. |
| (o)(3)(iii)(B)(*8*) | "Evidence that the alien has either commanded a high salary or will command a high salary or other remuneration for services, evidenced by contracts or other reliable evidence" | **One optional criterion of eight**; a petitioner needs any three. Not a pay floor. |
| (o)(14) | Strike/labor-dispute provision — petition denied if employment "would adversely affect the **wages** and working conditions of U.S. citizens" | Applies only during a certified work stoppage. |

There is **no** labor condition application, **no** prevailing wage, **no** required-wage
obligation, and **no** rule against non-productive or unpaid status in the O regulations.

### 2.4 The contrast that proves the point: H-1B has the rule, O-1 does not

DOL's H-1B "no benching" rule, 20 CFR 655.731(c)(7)(i):

> "If the H-1B nonimmigrant is not performing work and is in a nonproductive status due to a
> decision by the employer (e.g., because of lack of assigned work), lack of a permit or
> license, or any other reason except as specified in paragraph (c)(7)(ii) of this section, the
> employer shall pay the nonimmigrant the full amount of the weekly salary..."

That obligation exists because the H-1B program is built on an LCA wage attestation
(INA 212(n); 20 CFR 655.731). The O-1 program has no LCA and no counterpart provision. Where
Congress and DOL wanted a guaranteed-pay rule, they wrote one; its absence from the O
scheme is the strongest structural evidence available.

### 2.5 The contract is the governing document, and you write it

8 CFR 214.2(o)(2)(ii)(B) — the evidence that must accompany the petition:

> "Copies of any written contracts between the petitioner and the alien beneficiary or, if
> there is no written contract, **a summary of the terms of the oral agreement** under which the
> alien will be employed"

USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2, Part M, Ch. 7 (Documentation and Evidence), on that summary:

> "The terms offered by the petitioner (employer); and the terms accepted by the beneficiary
> (employee)."
> "The summary does not have to be signed by both parties to establish the oral agreement."

Consequence: **a deferral that is written into the employment agreement is not a deviation
from the terms — it *is* the terms.** A petition filed (or amended) on an agreement that says
"$X salary, accrued and payable on [trigger], plus [equity]" is a petition whose wage terms
USCIS has approved.

## 3. What actually constrains a deferral

### 3.1 Material change → amended petition

8 CFR 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(D):

> "**Amended petition.** The petitioner shall file an amended petition on the form prescribed by
> USCIS, with fee, to reflect **any material changes in the terms and conditions of employment**
> or the beneficiary's eligibility as specified in the original approved petition."

USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2, Part M, Ch. 3 (Petitioners): the petitioner must "immediately
notify USCIS of any changes in the terms and conditions of employment" that could affect
eligibility. USCIS's O Q&A repeats it: "In cases where there have been any material changes
in the terms and conditions of the employment or the beneficiary's eligibility, as specified
in the original I-129, an amended I-129 petition must be filed."

There is **no regulatory definition of "material"** for O petitions and no USCIS list.
Practitioner consensus (e.g., Scott Legal, P.C., analyzing exactly this question) runs:

- **Temporary** deferral, duties and hours unchanged, eligibility unaffected → good argument
  that no material change occurred and no amended petition is required.
- **Long-term or permanent**, or a large reduction (their example: 80%) → file the amended
  petition.
- If the original petition **argued the high-salary criterion**, any reduction is more
  dangerous, because it can undercut the basis on which the classification was approved.

**Practical rule:** the safest posture is that the deferral is documented *in advance*, is
time-limited with a defined payment trigger, leaves duties/hours/title untouched, and — if
long-term — is papered through an amended I-129 filed before it takes effect.

### 3.2 Don't knock out your own eligibility basis

If the approved petition satisfied 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(B)(*8*) (high salary/remuneration),
deferring cash can be read as eliminating that criterion. Two mitigations:

- Establish and document the other criteria (only three of eight are needed) so the
  classification does not depend on pay.
- Use 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(C) — "comparable evidence" — and value the **total**
  remuneration package: accrued-and-payable salary (a real liability the company owes you),
  equity at a defensible valuation (409A report, cap table, share certificates or executed
  SAFEs — not unexercised options), bonus rights, and benefits.

Note for downstream filings: the EB-1A analogue, 8 CFR 204.5(h)(3)(ix) ("commanded a high
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the
field"), is likewise **one optional criterion of ten**. A deferral period does not sink an
EB-1A; it just means that criterion should not be the one you lean on for those years.

### 3.3 The employment must remain real

Deferral is defensible; *cessation* is not. Under the Policy Manual, Vol. 2, Part M, Ch. 9,
USCIS "does not consider a beneficiary in O-1 status to have failed to maintain nonimmigrant
status solely because of the cessation of the employment on which the visa classification was
based for a period of up to 60 days or until the end of the authorized validity period,
whichever is shorter" — i.e., the clock the deferral must never be mistaken for. Keep
performing the petitioned services, for the petitioner, on the approved terms.

Also live during any deferral: 8 CFR 214.2(o)(16), which makes the employer and petitioner
"jointly and severally liable for the reasonable cost of return transportation" if employment
terminates other than by voluntary resignation.

One thing that is *not* a risk: pursuing a green card in parallel. 8 CFR 214.2(o)(13) provides
that "[t]he approval of a permanent labor certification or the filing of a preference petition
for an alien shall not be a basis for denying an O-1 petition, a request to extend such a
petition, or the alien's application for admission, change of status, or extension of stay."

### 3.4 Anticipate the paper trail an adjudicator will see

At extension, change of employer, consular renewal, or a later I-485, a **$0 or near-$0 W-2**
is the fact that invites the RFE — not the deferral itself. The answer is to have the
contemporaneous documents in Part 5 already assembled, so the record shows a funded,
board-approved, accrued obligation rather than an abandoned job.

## 4. The bigger risk is wage-and-hour law, not USCIS

Immigration law lets you defer. Employment law may not, and this is where founders get hurt.

- **Minimum-wage rights generally cannot be waived by agreement.** An "I agree to work
  unpaid" or "I agree to defer" clause does not, by itself, defeat a later minimum-wage or
  timely-payment claim under the FLSA or state law (e.g., California Labor Code §§ 204, 221;
  New York Labor Law art. 6).
- **The business-owner exemption is the usual path for founders.** 29 CFR 541.101 exempts
  "any employee who owns at least a bona fide **20-percent equity interest** in the enterprise
  in which the employee is employed... and who is **actively engaged in its management**," and
  states that "[t]he requirements of Subpart G (salary requirements) of this part do not apply
  to the business owners described in this section." State law does not always match — check
  the state of employment.
- **Deferred compensation is a tax instrument.** A promise to pay later can be nonqualified
  deferred compensation under IRC § 409A, with penalty exposure if the payment trigger and
  election timing are wrong. Have the deferral drafted with 409A in mind (short-term deferral
  exception, or a compliant fixed payment event).
- If you are a salaried employee **without** meaningful equity/management authority, a pure
  deferral is the hardest version of this to do lawfully, regardless of what USCIS thinks.

## 5. Evidence to build now (the record that makes this defensible)

Assemble these contemporaneously — reconstructed paperwork is what draws scrutiny:

1. **Written deferral agreement or addendum** to the employment agreement, signed before the
   deferral starts, stating: employment continues; title, duties, hours, and worksite
   unchanged; the deferred amount; that it **accrues as a company liability**; the payment
   trigger (date, financing close, revenue milestone); and interest terms if any.
2. **Board (or compensation committee) written consent** approving the deferral — ideally with
   an independent director or investor signatory.
3. **Accounting proof it is a real obligation**: the accrued liability on the balance sheet,
   payroll-provider records, and any promissory note.
4. **Continuity of the employment relationship**: active payroll registration, continued
   benefits enrollment, I-9 on file, org chart, work product, internal communications, and
   pay stubs / W-2s covering whatever *is* being paid.
5. **Employer support letter** stating that the beneficiary continues to perform the services
   described in the approved petition, that the deferral is [temporary / structural], and that
   the company remains obligated to pay the accrued amount.
6. **Total-remuneration valuation** if equity carries the compensation story: 409A valuation
   report, cap table, executed share/SAFE documents.
7. **Attorney memo on materiality** — and, if the conclusion is "material," the **amended
   Form I-129** filed with the deferral agreement attached, before the change takes effect.
8. **409A / wage-and-hour opinion** from employment-and-tax counsel.

---

## 6. Candor about the limits of this research

No USCIS policy statement, precedent decision, or AAO decision located in this research
*expressly* holds that deferred compensation is permissible on an O-1. The case is built from
(a) USCIS's express "no particular wage structure is required," (b) the absence of any payment
obligation across the whole of 8 CFR 214.2(o), (c) the H-1B contrast, and (d) the
contract-terms framework in (o)(2)(ii)(B) and the Policy Manual. That is a strong structural
argument, not a safe harbor. The material-change question in Part 3.1 is genuinely
fact-specific and is where counsel's judgment is needed.
